01 · For teachers

Ready-to-use lesson plans

Three modular lessons build on activities already available in the Learning Lab. Suggested age: 14+. For ages 12–14, shorten source extracts, pre-teach vocabulary and provide closer teacher guidance. These are adaptable plans, not a substitute for checking the original sources.

Lesson 1 · Evidence

What can a photograph tell us?

Essential question: How do historians distinguish what an image shows from what its context helps us understand?

45 minutesStarter 5 · Observe 8 · Context 12 · Group analysis 12 · Exit reflection 8
90 minutesStarter 10 · Observe 15 · Context 20 · Group analysis 25 · Compare sources 12 · Reflection 8

Learning objectives

  • Separate direct observation from interpretation and contextual knowledge.
  • Identify the role of caption, date, creator, archive and provenance.
  • State at least one limitation of a photograph as historical evidence.

Preparation and materials: Open The Evidence Desk and the linked USHMM photograph record. Display the image with its original caption and record. Do not crop away contextual information.

Discussion prompts: What is literally visible? What does the caption add? What remains unknown? Whose perspective is represented? What other source could corroborate the interpretation?

Teacher guidance: Do not ask students to imagine themselves as victims or to invent dialogue. If a student makes a claim about identity, motive or emotion from appearance alone, ask what evidence would be needed to support it.

Your task

  1. Write three details you can directly observe.
  2. Write two questions the image cannot answer by itself.
  3. Read the archive caption and record what new context it provides.
  4. Write one careful claim and name the evidence that supports it.
Lesson 2 · Stories & memory

One testimony is not every story

Essential question: How can an individual account help us understand history without treating one person's experience as universal?

45 minutesOpening 5 · Read source 10 · Context 10 · Pair work 12 · Reflection 8
90 minutesOpening 10 · Source reading 15 · Context 15 · Compare records 20 · Object/label task 20 · Reflection 10

Learning objectives

  • Recognise testimony or diary writing as a situated individual source.
  • Distinguish an individual's account from broader historical generalisation.
  • Connect personal accounts with contextual and administrative records without allowing one to erase the other.

Preparation: Use the published Łódź ghetto diary excerpt in The Evidence Desk, alongside its USHMM context page. Explain the date, place and circumstances of the record. Do not infer a complete biography from a short excerpt.

Discussion prompts: What does the writer choose to record? What might the source not tell us? How does the historical context deepen our reading? What other voices or records would broaden the picture?

Teacher guidance: Avoid asking learners to role-play a person in the ghetto. Offer a quiet alternative to reading aloud. Do not turn testimony into a guessing game or ask students to rank suffering.

Your task

  1. Identify the date, place and type of source.
  2. Underline one detail that is explicitly stated.
  3. Write one question that remains unanswered.
  4. Explain why this testimony matters and why it cannot represent every person's experience.
Lesson 3 · Comparative history

Three countries, different records

Essential question: What do archives reveal about different local histories, and why must we avoid treating countries as if they had one uniform experience?

45 minutesMap warm-up 5 · Source stations 18 · Compare 14 · Exit ticket 8
90 minutesMap warm-up 10 · Source stations 30 · Context research 20 · Comparison 20 · Reflection 10

Learning objectives

  • Compare different types of records and identify what each can establish.
  • Distinguish pre-war Bulgarian territory from Bulgarian-administered occupied territories during the war.
  • Recognise that Romanian policy and persecution varied across time and regions.

Preparation: Use the Three Countries, Different Records source lab. Provide the original archive records, not isolated images alone. Students should record institution, record type, date, place and limits.

Discussion prompts: Why might a police file and a photograph preserve different traces? Who created each record and for what purpose? What is absent? How does geography change the historical question?

Teacher guidance: The USHMM record about deportations from Lom in March 1943 concerns Jews deported from Bulgarian-occupied territories, including Macedonia and Thrace; do not generalise this event to all Jews in Bulgaria's pre-war core provinces. For Romania, use the archive record alongside regional and chronological context. Avoid national character explanations.

Your task

  1. Choose one source from each country.
  2. For each, note creator/institution, date, place and source type.
  3. Write one fact the source supports and one question it cannot answer.
  4. Compare how the records differ and explain why the contexts matter.
Safeguarding and classroom climate: Give advance notice of difficult content, avoid graphic imagery unless there is a clear pedagogical reason, do not compel personal disclosure, and provide a way to pause or step out. Correct antisemitic stereotypes and denial clearly; do not frame them as equally valid opinions.

03 · Key language

Glossary

Short working definitions. Terms should always be read in their historical context; some have legal, scholarly or institution-specific meanings.

Ghetto
In the Holocaust, an area where German authorities and their allies forcibly concentrated Jews, often under extreme overcrowding, hunger, disease and restrictions. Ghettos varied in form, purpose and duration.
Deportation
The forced removal and transport of people from one place to another by state or occupying authorities. During the Holocaust, deportations often led to ghettos, forced labour sites or killing centres.
Collaboration
Cooperation with an occupying or persecuting regime. Its forms, motives and degrees varied; the term should be applied to documented actions and specific contexts, not used as a blanket label for a population.
Resistance
Actions opposing persecution or Nazi rule. Resistance could be armed or unarmed, individual or collective, cultural, spiritual, educational, documentary, mutual-aid or escape-related.
Testimony
An account given by a person about experiences or events they witnessed or lived through. It is a vital historical source, read with attention to context, memory, circumstances and corroboration.
Perpetrator
A person who planned, enabled or carried out persecution, dispossession, deportation or murder. Historical analysis distinguishes roles, levels of authority and documented actions.
Witness / bystander
A witness observes or knows about events; “bystander” is often used for people or institutions not directly targeted or perpetrating, whose responses ranged from assistance to passivity or support. These categories require context.
Victim / survivor
A victim is a person targeted by persecution or violence. A survivor is someone who lived through it. Neither term should reduce a person to what was done to them; use names and biographies where documented.
Memory
How individuals and communities remember, interpret and commemorate the past. Public memory is shaped by testimony, archives, education, politics, monuments and changing social contexts.
Antisemitism
Prejudice, hostility or discrimination against Jews as Jews. Nazi antisemitism combined older religious and racialised ideas with conspiracy myths and state policy, and became central to persecution and genocide.
04 · Historical thinking

How to work with a difficult source

A source does not “speak for itself”. Its meaning depends on who created it, when, why, for whom, how it survived and what other evidence can confirm or complicate it.

  1. Identify the source. Is it a photograph, official document, map, diary, testimony, object, newspaper or later memorial? Record creator, date, place, archive and catalogue reference where available.
  2. Separate observation from inference. Describe what is visibly or explicitly present before interpreting it. Mark uncertainty rather than filling gaps with imagination.
  3. Ask who made it and why. An administrative document may reflect the categories and goals of the institution that produced it. A photograph may be staged, cropped, captioned later or made for propaganda.
  4. Read the caption and provenance critically. Captions can be contemporary or added later. Check whether the archive identifies photographer, date, location and original context.
  5. Notice absence and limits. What cannot this source tell you? Whose perspective is missing? Does the source represent one person, one institution, a selected moment or a broader pattern?
  6. Corroborate. Compare with another independent source: testimony, record, map, photograph, scholarly account or other archival item. Agreement and disagreement both need explanation.
  7. Make a careful claim. Use language such as “this source shows…”, “the archive identifies…” or “the evidence suggests…”. Distinguish established fact from interpretation and open question.

Photographs

Do not infer identity, emotion, consent or motive from appearance alone. Check photographer, date, caption, location, purpose and whether the image was cropped or reused.

Propaganda

Analyse the intended audience, stereotypes, omissions, emotional techniques and institutional purpose. Do not repeat dehumanising claims without clear framing and critique.

Administrative records

Ask who created the categories, what administrative task the record served and whose lives were reduced to entries, numbers or lists. Bureaucratic language is not neutral.

Testimony and diaries

Respect the speaker's words and circumstances. Avoid treating one testimony as universal or demanding that a survivor's memory function like a complete administrative record.

Ethical practice: Never ask students to role-play victims, perpetrators or historical suffering as entertainment. Do not invent quotations, biographies or “missing voices”. If a creative response is used, label it clearly as contemporary student work.
