HOLOCAUST EDUCATION PROJECT
Learning Lab — teacher notes
Updated: source-based learning activities

OVERVIEW
The Learning Lab complements the partner-created Holocaust History Timeline activity. It now includes five learning areas across the five handbook pathways:
1. The Evidence Desk — analyse a law, a diary, and a photograph/caption.
2. Choices Under Pressure — examine coercion, unequal power, and constrained decisions during the September 1942 deportations from the Łódź ghetto.
3. Pause Before You Share — analyse propaganda in its historical context and practise responsible verification of contemporary information.\n4. Stories — object-based inquiry, statistics alongside individual biographies/testimonies, and evidence-informed museum labels.\n5. Memory — local Jewish history source investigation, memorial analysis and a historically grounded memorial design brief.\nThe Today pathway is also extended with The Power of Words, Letters Across Time, Standing Up Against Hate and From Memory to Action.

Suggested age: 14+ for the full discussion, with teacher adaptation for younger groups. Some questions can be completed by learners aged 12–14 with guidance. The project's age range should be stated consistently across the handbook, website, and lesson plans.

HISTORICAL SOURCES AND ATTRIBUTION
The activity uses a clearly labelled adapted summary and a short excerpt from a published diary transcription, alongside an archival photograph and source record. The visual quiz uses the museum-hosted image URL and links learners to the original record; it does not claim ownership of the image. Check the museum's current terms and permissions before reusing the image in other publications. Sources include these United States Holocaust Memorial Museum (USHMM) resources:
• “Nuremberg Race Laws”: https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/nuremberg-laws
• “‘Give Me Your Children’: Voices from the Lodz Ghetto”: https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/give-me-your-children-voices-from-the-lodz-ghetto
• “Teaching Materials Using Primary Sources and the Museum’s Collections”: https://www.ushmm.org/teach/teaching-materials/primary-sources-collections
• “Primary Source Databases”: https://www.ushmm.org/collections/plan-a-research-visit/electronic-resources/primary-source-databases

The source cards explicitly label the historical text as an adapted summary, not a direct quotation. The site links to the museum's full historical account and context. Before formal educational use, check links and the museum's current usage terms. Do not copy or reproduce archival images without checking rights and permissions.

ACTIVITY 1 — THE EVIDENCE DESK
Learning objectives:
• distinguish a law's text from evidence of how it was enforced in a particular place;
• interpret a diary as evidence of one person's experience without generalising to everyone;
• understand how captions, provenance, and corroborating records help interpret photographs;
• formulate a claim and state what a source cannot establish on its own.
Format: five interactive questions with explanatory feedback, followed by a written claim. The expanded visual investigation uses an archival USHMM photograph, a revealable caption/context panel, and seven claims to classify as directly visible, confirmed by the archive record, or not inferable from the available evidence.
Suggested time: 15–20 minutes.
Follow-up: Ask learners to choose one source and complete: “This source helps us understand… because… However, it cannot tell us…”. Invite them to identify another source that could test or extend the claim.

ACTIVITY 2 — CHOICES UNDER PRESSURE
Learning objectives:
• identify who held coercive power during the September 1942 deportations from the Łódź ghetto;
• distinguish constrained action from a free choice;
• discuss institutional roles without erasing Nazi responsibility or treating the Jewish population as a single voice;
• identify whose perspectives and what additional records are needed.
Format: five interactive case questions with explanatory feedback, followed by a written reflection.
Suggested time: 15–20 minutes.
Facilitation note: Do not ask students to decide whether people in the ghetto made “good” or “bad” choices from a safe distance. Keep German occupation, threats, imprisonment, and the extreme lack of safe options in view. Avoid simplistic hero/villain framing. Invite careful evidence-based discussion of institutional roles and the range of experiences.

ACTIVITY 3 — PAUSE BEFORE YOU SHARE
Learning objectives:
• identify stereotypes and claims that lack verifiable evidence;
• trace an image or claim to its original source;
• analyse propaganda in relation to audience, purpose, historical context, and state policy;
• compare communication techniques without equating distinct historical situations;
• choose responsible responses to harmful online content.
Format: five interactive questions with explanatory feedback, followed by a verification plan.
Suggested time: 15–20 minutes.
Follow-up: Learners write three checks they would make before sharing an uncited historical image.

SAFE AND RESPECTFUL CLASSROOM USE
• Preview the materials and set expectations for respectful discussion.
• Explain that the Holocaust involved systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder; propaganda was one part of a wider system that included law, institutions, dispossession, deportation, and mass murder.
• Do not ask learners to search for hateful content, reproduce slurs, or disclose personal experiences of discrimination.
• Make space for learners to pause or ask questions.
• Acknowledge that testimonies are individual accounts and that not every victim or survivor had the same experience.
• Use historical photographs and testimony with context; avoid using traumatic material merely to provoke shock.
• Check all sources, translations, captions, and rights before adapting the activities for publication or classroom handouts.

TECHNICAL NOTES
• The activities are static HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. No student accounts, tracking, analytics, or third-party JavaScript libraries are used.
• Learner answers in the reflection boxes are not sent to a server; they remain in the open browser page and are not saved when the page is closed or refreshed.
• Keyboard focus indicators and immediate written feedback are included. The activities are not timed.
• The chronology activity is hosted externally by the project partner in Romania.


ADDITIONAL HANDBOOK-ALIGNED LEARNING TASKS
STORIES — A SUITCASE, A PHOTOGRAPH, A NAME
The online task follows the handbook's object-based inquiry: observe material details; formulate questions and cautious hypotheses; then check the museum/archive record and provenance. The object alone does not establish its owner's thoughts or complete life story. The extension “Behind Every Number Is a Story” asks learners to consider what aggregate statistics can show and what an individual biography or testimony can add. Teachers must select and cite a verified statistic and a documented individual account; clarify estimates versus exact counts. The museum-label task requires at least two source-supported details, a stated limitation and an exact source reference. Imagined writing must never be presented as authentic testimony.

MEMORY — MEMORY DETECTIVES, MONUMENTS SPEAK, DESIGNING A MEMORIAL
The local-history investigation uses teacher-selected maps, photographs, building/cemetery records, museum objects, newspaper extracts or oral-history records. Students record source title, creator/institution, date (if known), and location. They distinguish documented facts from hypotheses and identify what further evidence is needed. The memorial analysis asks who is commemorated, who created the memorial, when and where, what the design communicates, and what it leaves out. The design brief requires historical evidence, a reasoned location, accessible interpretation and clear limits; students must not invent names or personal histories.

TODAY — THE POWER OF WORDS, LETTERS ACROSS TIME, FROM MEMORY TO ACTION
The language exercise uses neutral fictional examples for practice, followed by a teacher-selected and verified historical source. Analyse creator, audience, purpose, emotional techniques, in-group/out-group framing, omissions and historical context. Do not reproduce slurs for shock value. The letter task is a reflective writing exercise based on a documented source; it must not claim to speak for a victim or invent historical quotations. “From Memory to Action” asks for a specific, realistic civic or heritage action grounded in the learning.

All new tasks are structured templates where the handbook requires teachers to select a local or individual source. The website does not fabricate a named object, local resident, memorial record or testimony. Teachers should supply and cite the exact verified source before classroom use.


ADDITIONAL ACTIVITIES ADDED TO THE LEARNING LAB
CHOICES — THE BYSTANDER'S DILEMMA
The new activity follows the handbook lesson on bystander behaviour. It introduces perpetrators, collaborators, bystanders and rescuers, and asks learners to investigate knowledge, position, social pressure, fear, perceived risk, available options and consequences. Its interactive case is a methodological prompt, not a claim about a specific historical individual. A teacher must select and cite a documented historical case before using the activity for case-based discussion. Do not assume that all bystanders had the same knowledge or opportunity to intervene; understanding circumstances is not the same as excusing consequences.

STORIES — CHILDREN'S VOICES: “THE BUTTERFLY”
The new interdisciplinary prompt follows the handbook's lesson on Pavel Friedmann's poem “The Butterfly” (1942) in the context of the Terezín ghetto. Learners analyse imagery, contrast and symbolism, then connect the poem to verified historical context. The website does not reproduce the poem. Teachers must use a verified edition and reliable biographical/historical sources. Any student-created poem, visual work or sound response must be labelled as a contemporary creative response, never as Friedmann's words or testimony. A single poem is one particular voice, not a representative account of all children.

EVIDENCE — IF THESE WALLS COULD SPEAK
The new activity adapts the handbook's method of reading buildings, photographs and objects as historical evidence. The handbook's worked example concerns the Sofia Synagogue and the history of Bulgarian Jews during the Second World War. The online task explicitly invites teachers to adapt the method to a documented local site. Supply verified dates, architect, purpose, historical context and source references before classroom use. A photograph of a building cannot establish a complete community history or prove that a building remained unchanged.

The new tasks complement the existing five pathways and are designed as short digital entry points, not replacements for the handbook's full 50- or 90-minute lesson plans. The site's general age recommendation remains 14+; adapt materials for younger learners only with teacher guidance and age-appropriate sources.
