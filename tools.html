Three source-based activities for careful historical thinking. Read the evidence, test interpretations, and explain what you can—and cannot—conclude.

For teachers and learners. These activities use short, clearly labelled adaptations of information from historical sources and museum research. Adaptations are not presented as original quotations. The Holocaust was the systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators. Other groups were also persecuted and murdered. Treat testimony and historical records with care; do not rank suffering or judge people under coercion as if they had free choices.

The Evidence Desk

Historians do not ask only whether a source is “true” or “false”. They ask who made it, why it exists, what it can establish, what it leaves out, and how it fits with other evidence.

Your task: examine three historical source files. For each one, choose the strongest interpretation supported by the evidence.

5 investigations · approx. 15 minutes
Source file A · LawGermany · 15 September 1935

The Nuremberg Laws

Carefully adapted source summary — not a quotation

Two laws passed by Nazi Germany stripped Jews of full Reich citizenship and prohibited marriages between Jews and people classified as being of “German or related blood”. The laws made antisemitic racial ideology part of state law. The law text describes what the regime authorised and prohibited; by itself, it does not document every local act of enforcement or every person’s experience.

Source: United States Holocaust Memorial Museum (USHMM), “Nuremberg Race Laws”. Read the historical context and translated law ↗

Source file B · DiaryŁódź ghetto · March 1942

A young diarist writes about hunger

Carefully adapted source summary — not a quotation

An anonymous girl diarist in the Łódź ghetto recorded severe shortages of food and the daily struggle to find something to eat. In a separate entry the next day, she described how a sunny day briefly lifted her mood, even while the ghetto fence made her feel trapped. These entries give evidence of one young person’s experience and of the coexistence of hardship, feeling, and ordinary daily observation.

Source: USHMM, “‘Give Me Your Children’: Voices from the Lodz Ghetto”. Read the diary extracts and historical context ↗

Source file C · Photograph and captionŁódź ghetto · September 1942

A photograph of deportation

Carefully adapted source summary — not a quotation

A USHMM collection entry identifies a photograph showing Jewish children being deported from the Łódź ghetto during the German authorities’ September 1942 roundup. Its caption supplies a place, date, historical event, and collection credit. The image is evidence, but the caption and related records are essential to identify the event responsibly; a photograph alone cannot tell us every person’s name, thoughts, or later fate.

Source: USHMM, “‘Give Me Your Children’: Voices from the Lodz Ghetto”. Read the photograph caption and context ↗

Investigation complete

Historian’s habit: make claims no stronger than the evidence allows. Uncertainty is not failure; it tells you what to investigate next.

Build an evidence-based claim

Choose one source above. Complete this sentence in your own words: “This source helps us understand ______ because ______. However, it cannot tell us ______.”

Use this historian’s checklist:
  • Have you named a specific detail in the source?
  • Have you explained how that detail supports your claim?
  • Have you identified something the source cannot establish on its own?
  • Have you avoided turning one person’s experience into a claim about everyone?

There is no single model sentence. Strong answers make a clear claim, use evidence precisely, and state a real limitation.

Pathways · Choices · Stories

Choices Under Pressure

This case study asks how we analyse decisions when power is radically unequal. The purpose is not to decide whether people in the ghetto made “good” or “bad” choices from a safe distance. It is to identify who held power, what options were constrained or removed, and whose perspectives are needed to understand an event.

5 case questions · approx. 15 minutes
Historical case fileŁódź ghetto · September 1942

German authorities demand deportations

Historical summary based on USHMM research

In early September 1942, German authorities ordered that 20,000 people be deported from the Łódź ghetto, including children, older people, and the sick. The German authorities held the decisive coercive power. Chaim Rumkowski, head of the Jewish Council, announced the demand to the ghetto population on 4 September. During the roundup, German SS and police forces carried out deportations; people who tried to hide or flee faced extreme danger, and more than 500 Jews were murdered during the operation.

The situation was shaped by German occupation, imprisonment, starvation, threats, and the removal of meaningful freedom. The role of the Jewish Council and Rumkowski has been debated, but analysis must begin with the coercive system imposed by the Nazi authorities and the fact that the people targeted were victims of persecution.

Source: USHMM, “‘Give Me Your Children’: Voices from the Lodz Ghetto”. Read the full account and further questions ↗

Before you begin: distinguish three things

  • Power: who created and enforced the threat?
  • Constraint: which options were blocked by violence, imprisonment, or the threat of collective punishment?
  • Perspective: whose testimony or records do we need before drawing a conclusion?
CASE QUESTION 1 OF 5Score: 0

Case study complete

Remember: analysing constrained decisions is not the same as blaming people who were persecuted. Historical responsibility depends on power, actions, intent, and context.

Discussion: how should historians write about constrained choices?

In pairs, discuss two questions. Try to support each point with a detail from the case file.

  1. Why is it misleading to describe the September 1942 roundup as a free choice made by the ghetto population?
  2. What additional sources would help us understand the event from the perspectives of parents, children, German officials, Jewish Council members, and people who tried to hide?
  3. How can a historian examine Rumkowski’s actions without ignoring the power of the German authorities or treating the Jewish population as a single voice?
A strong discussion answer:
  • names the German authorities’ power and coercion;
  • recognises that people in the ghetto did not have equal power or safe options;
  • does not treat the Jewish population as one uniform group;
  • distinguishes documented facts from interpretation and identifies evidence still needed.
Pathways · Today · Evidence

Pause Before You Share

Learn to examine a message without being pulled along by its emotional force. First analyse historical propaganda in context; then practise checking claims online. Some techniques can recur across time, but similarities in technique do not make different events historically equivalent.

5 investigations · approx. 15 minutes
Historical context fileNazi Germany · 1935 onward

When prejudice becomes state policy

Historical summary — not a quotation from a propaganda poster

Nazi antisemitism did not consist only of insulting messages or individual prejudice. The regime used propaganda and racial stereotypes while also changing laws, excluding Jews from citizenship and public life, encouraging social exclusion, and using state institutions to enforce persecution. The 1935 Nuremberg Laws are one example of antisemitic ideology being turned into law.

Source: USHMM, “Nuremberg Race Laws”. Read the legal and historical context ↗

Investigation complete

Verification routine: pause → identify the original source → check date and context → compare independent reliable sources → decide whether sharing is useful and safe.

Apply the verification routine

Imagine a post claims that a historical photograph proves a dramatic event, but gives no date, location, photographer, or archive reference. Write down the first three checks you would make before sharing it.

Look for these steps:
  • find the original image or document and the holding institution;
  • check the caption, date, location, creator, and any later edits or reposts;
  • compare the claim with reliable, independent sources;
  • avoid reposting harmful or misleading material while checking.

Discussion: historical propaganda and today's information environment

  • What is the difference between a stereotype and a specific, verifiable claim?
  • Why is “everyone is sharing it” not evidence that a claim is true?
  • How can we compare propaganda techniques across time without claiming that every modern dispute is equivalent to the Holocaust?
